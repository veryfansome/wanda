# Memory: the plan, and where the work actually is

`memories.md` says what memory is for and collects the interactions it has to serve. It is the north star and it is not a design document — it is the record of the two of us groping out how wanda should behave, and it gets edited when intent changes, not when a prototype disagrees with it.

This document is the other half: what we build, why the pieces are shaped the way they are, what the lab has actually established, and what is next. If you are a new session, read `memories.md` first for what we are trying to build, then this for what exists and what is known, then `lab/CLAUDE.md` if you are going to run anything.

A file-and-line reference in this document names the Python that was the implementation when the finding was made — `lab/store.py`, `lab/run.py`. The store and the `mem` CLI are Rust now, in `memory/`, and the harness in `lab/harness/`. What was measured still holds; the line number is where it was found, not where it lives.

## Start here

- **`docs/memories.md`** — the intent. What memory is for, fourteen interactions it has to serve, parked ideas, open questions. Authored with the owner. Treat it as the target, not as a spec to check the code against.
- **This document** — the method, the design decisions that survived and why, what has been measured, what is next.
- **`docs/recall-corpus.md`** — the instrument. Forty-five scenes, 141 inputs, 54 checkpoints, seven months of household history. All run; 44 and 45 first in round 12.
- **`lab/README.md`** — how to run the thing.
- **`docs/issues.md`** — thirty-four defects found by an adversarial review after round 15, each reproduced before being written down. Two of them mean the bands recorded here are not comparable across the fix: the judge scored against the final vault rather than the store each checkpoint met, and recall depended on the order its arguments were given.

Rounds 1 through 4 have been squashed into this document. Their write-ups are gone from `docs/` deliberately: rounds 1 and 2 were measured on a harness with defects that invalidate their numbers, and keeping four chronological write-ups made a reader reconstruct a research journey to find out what is currently true. What survives from each round is below, corrected. The originals are in git history if anyone ever needs them.

## How we are working

Small critical pieces, built as prototypes, judged against what we actually want, then reworked. Not a comprehensive design document first.

The reason is that most of what is still open cannot be settled by reading. How far association travels before it turns into noise, what tunes ranking, what is worth keeping, what a promotion bar should be: these are empirical questions, and a week of prototype answers them better than any amount of careful prose. The previous attempt on `main` was designed thoroughly on paper and was still wrong in the direction that mattered.

So each round is: pick a piece, build the smallest version that can be exercised end to end, run it against real situations, read what it did, and say plainly how far it is from the intent. Then either fix it or throw it away. Pieces get to be rough for a long time.

**What "validated" means.** Against an interaction from `memories.md`, end to end, not against the piece's own success criterion. Not "recall works" but "the childcare gap interaction is servable", including the part where the fact was captured in a conversation weeks before the newsletter arrived. This matters because iterating on pieces until each is locally good is how you end up with pieces that do not compose. The interactions are the only thing that spans them.

Recall in particular cannot be scored on hit rate, since a partial hit that provokes a good question is a success by design. What can be judged is whether something useful came back and whether it came back buried. That judgment needs a fixed set of situations, which is what the corpus is.

**How to read a run.** Four runs is a noisy smoke test and nothing more. It cannot measure an effect size, rank two conditions, or establish that a scene is exhausted, and treating it as measurement is how this document came to assert things it could not support. What the corpus is for is staging situations realistic enough to show how wanda behaves in use.

A scene earns its place by setting up a situation worth testing. It does not have to return the same verdict every time, and a stable verdict is not evidence that it has stopped being useful.

Two things in a run are worth acting on.

*A structurally wrong scenario.* An expectation that cannot be met, one that names a distractor the store does not hold yet, one contradicted by its own scene — these are broken tests and they should be fixed. `lab/lint.py` exists for exactly this, and it is the only part of the pipeline whose output is a defect rather than an observation.

*Behaviour that does not happen when it is needed.* This is the telling signal, and a handful of runs is enough to see it. If something wanda has to be relied on for fires in two runs of four, that is a problem — not because two of four is statistically significant, but because a behaviour that appears half the time cannot be depended on. The bar for dependable is near four of four, which is why so few runs can screen for it while being useless for everything else.

So read a scored set per expectation, not per total. Round 5's 63 shoulds came out 37 reliable at 4/4, 14 firing somewhere between 1 and 3 times, and 12 never firing at all. The middle band is the work list, and the twelve that never fire say a capability is missing rather than unreliable.

**When a prototype and `memories.md` disagree, that is a finding to report, not a licence to edit the intent.** The document is the owner's, and a measurement taken on a harness we have already replaced twice is weak evidence about what wanda should be. Say what the piece did and how far it is from the intent; changing the intent is the owner's call.

## The design decisions that survived, and why

**The model drives.** This is the one inversion everything else rests on. Round 1 was a Python pipeline that called the model as an extraction subroutine — capture turned an input into structured memory, recall did landing and traversal and ranking, and the model was a component inside both. That was backwards, and it was thrown out. Now every corpus input is one Claude Code session with the vault as its working directory: it reads the root index, navigates or calls `mem` as it sees fit, records what it judges worth recording, and reports what it brought to bear. Landing is the session's judgment. Traversal and ranking stay mechanical, because those are graph arithmetic and there is nothing for judgment to add.

This is not only an aesthetic preference. Three of round 1's four headline failures — hub saturation, full-text poisoning, and one real thing becoming three nodes — were artifacts of the pipeline and disappeared when the session picked its own seeds. A tokenizer that automatically seeds the speaker makes the speaker a hub; a session does not. That result is the reason not to go back, and it is the only thing worth keeping from round 1.

**The vault is the truth, the index is derived, the `CLAUDE.md` files are generated.** Memory is markdown with YAML-subset frontmatter. The SQLite+FTS index is rebuilt by reading those files back, so if the frontmatter did not carry enough, recall goes blind — that is a deliberate test of whether the file format is self-sufficient, and it has held.

**Only the root `CLAUDE.md` is pushed.** Measured directly: a session started in the vault sees the root marker and nothing else. After reading `people/jane.md` it sees the root and `people/`; `places/` never loads. The hierarchy is navigation-triggered, not ambient. The consequence is load-bearing and is what `lab/store.py` is built around — the root has to carry the standing instructions plus a map good enough to make a session go looking, and the generated directory indexes are the landing surface. If they do not send a session to the right place, nothing downstream can recover.

**A session's scope is the enclosing git repo, not its working directory.** This is the defect that voided rounds 1 and 2, and it is worth knowing before anyone builds on the harness. Claude Code resolves `CLAUDE.md` and auto-memory from the enclosing repo, and `--setting-sources project` does not change that. With vaults living at `lab/vaultA` inside this repo, every replay session loaded wanda's own project memory — and wrote to it. Five memories in this project's store were authored by replay sessions, in the first person, stamped with corpus dates; one of them instructed every session after it to prefer the real date over the simulated one, which is exactly what they then did. The fix is `git init` in the vault, which makes it its own project. Anything relative in the child's environment resolves inside the vault too, so `MEM_VAULT` and the log paths are absolute.

**The harness stamps the dates.** A simulated date given in the user prompt reads as something the user believes and loses to the real date in the system prompt, which reads as a fact about the world. It goes in `--append-system-prompt`, where it wins. Frontmatter is stamped by the tool rather than by the session, and `mem` rewrites the real date out of every string a session passes, at one choke point after argument parsing. Scenes 5, 6 and 8 are about elapsed time, so a session that thinks eight months have passed when the corpus says six weeks is answering a different question.

**Scoring is a model reading the answer against the intent.** Token overlap cannot judge "did it bring the right thing to bear" when the answer is prose — it failed answers that were better than the expectation asked for, and it passed one that had no cadence in it at all. `lab/judge.py` reads each checkpoint and returns `hit|partial|miss` for what should come back and `absent|present-justified|present-noise` for what should not. The distinction between a distractor that leaked and one surfaced deliberately is the thing the old scoring could not make. The judge is stable: the same run judged three times moved by one hit while two runs of the same configuration differed by seven.

**Every session snapshots the vault first.** A bare repo outside the vault takes a commit before each session, which makes one question askable that otherwise is not: when two runs disagree, is it the store or the session? Replaying one checkpoint N times against each frozen store varies only the session within a store, and only the store across them.

**Ids are opaque, names are labels, and the index line is the summary.** Through round 14 a node's id was a slug of its name — `event:2026-09-07-wanda-reminded-mei-about-the-end-of-term` — and the index line was the id, the name cut at sixty characters, and the body's first line cut at ninety: 198 characters a line, 144 lines in `events/CLAUDE.md` by the end of a run, and in 115 of them the tail repeated the name because the two truncations differed. A slug id also meant two things could not share a name, and a rename moved the file and rewrote every edge in the vault. Now an id is six hex characters, dated for an event, minted at creation; the name is a label that resolves by lookup and is refused when two nodes carry it; a rename changes the label and nothing else; every writer takes a one-line `--summary` of at most eighty characters (140 from round 18), refused over the cap rather than cut, and a `--body` that no index shows. A directory index line is the id without its kind, the marks, the name and the summary. Migrating a round-14 store: 40,329 index bytes to 17,408 with nothing lost, before any change to what gets filed.

**The store holds facts; the transcript holds exchanges.** The events directory had become the journal — "fan asked wanda who's down for the first week", "mei said morning wanda" — which is the transcript's job since round 11 and was the belt's before that. The standing behaviour now says so: an event is something that happened and stays true, a lunch, a result, a delivery, a suggestion she made — not a message; an action on an undertaking advances the trajectory and is not a second node; greetings, acknowledgements and the fact of having been asked earn nothing. What that does to the size of `events/` is measured in round 15.

**A second memory system was instructed at every turn.** Claude Code's own auto-memory is on in headless sessions — a bare `claude -p` gets it too — and it is 12,816 characters of the system prompt, nearly half, telling wanda to "build up this memory system over time so that future conversations can have a complete picture of who the user is" in a directory that is not the vault. Nothing ever wrote to it: the mounted directory is empty and the end-of-run check has never reported anything. But an instruction is a prior whether or not it is obeyed, which is the same defect as the corpus names below, and it sat in the cached prefix of every call at roughly ten dollars a round. `CLAUDE_CODE_DISABLE_AUTO_MEMORY=1` removes it, and every lab session now sets it.

**The corpus had got into the instructions, and one of them was the answer.** The standing behaviour and both skills are read by every session at every turn, and they had accumulated the corpus's own cast over five rounds: `mem recall Jane "Tony's"`, `mem search "car seat"`, `--object "Hartwell Trust"` in the root text; "don't tell fan about the lump", "the sitter and Dara", "fan getting hold of Robin, Robin returning the deposit" in the enrich skill; and in the retract skill, *"Jane is fan's sister" → "Jane is fan's cousin"* and *"Robin only answers texts" → "Robin answered mei's email the same day"* — scene 9 and scene 14, verbatim, as worked examples. Scene 9 scores 4/4 every round and that result cannot be trusted. Scene 14 is in the never band despite being handed its answer, which bounds how strong the priming is but clears nothing. `mem event --summary "suggested Sea of Tranquility to mei"` had been in the enrich skill since round 11, the round scene 42 was written for, and one run in four answered scene 42 with that title in both round 11 and round 15.

The fix is not better names: an example in a permanent instruction is a prior on every arrival, whatever it names. All three texts are now framework only — no cast, no worked scenarios, command templates with placeholders — and `lint.py`'s structural pass fails when a corpus name reaches any of them, a name being a word capitalised mid-sentence in the corpus and never written lowercase there, plus every speaker. Fifty-one names, none present, and a planted leak is caught.

**What wanda did is in the voice.** `--actor wanda` was meant to mark her own words and acts without making `person:wanda` a hub; sessions read the field name as "who did it" and filled it in for everyone, `[by fan]` thirty-six times in one round-15 store, which the `by-` tag then carried into Obsidian. The flag is gone rather than renamed: these are her memories, written by her, so an act with nobody named as doing it is hers, a named subject is someone else, and a happening has no actor. The round-15 evidence is that the field was never the retrieval mechanism anyway — scene 42 was four of four, found through `mem recall`, `mem search` and `mem session`, with the mark a label on the result rather than the thing that found it. Her commitments still reach `person:wanda` by edge, which is the part that works. Round 16 tests whether the voice holds what the field was standing in for.

**A node carries what its kind needs.** Read against a real store, the frontmatter of round 15's events was mostly the same fact written more than once or a field nothing read: `name` equal to `summary` in 105 of 105, `last_seen` equal to `created` in 105 of 105, `when` already in the id, and `permanence`, `stated`, `opened`, `closes` written by the tool and read by nobody — checked module by module. The writer now enforces a schema per kind: every node has a summary, `created`, `made` and `aka`; a named thing has `name` and `last_seen`; an event has `actor` and its date in its id; a thread has what it is waiting for; a rule has its type. `created` stays because it carries the one thing the id does not — when wanda learned it, which differs from when it happened in one event in twenty.

**An edge is a relation and a target.** Through round 15 an edge carried `since`, `source` and `permanence`, and a retraction marked it in place with a date and a reason — none of which anything read, and all of which made the frontmatter a flat string nobody could parse, Obsidian included. Now an edge is written as one list property per relation, `cousin_of: ["[[235b7e]]"]`, which is both the store's meaning and what Obsidian draws; provenance is the node's `made:` and the transcript; a retraction removes the edge, and the correction is recorded as the fact it is ("fan calls Jane his sister by mistake; she is his cousin"), because he will say it again. `mem forget` removes a node minted by mistake once nothing links to it, in place of renaming it to "stray node" and leaving it in every index, which a session did in round 15.

**A claim can be unsaid, and the file keeps the history.** Round 1 captured corrections and could not apply them: Jane's node held `sister-of` and `cousin-of` at once and answered with both. Round 3 was worse — a correction filed to `belt/`, which `build_index` skipped entirely, so run A kept a live `sibling_of` edge and answered accordingly. Both are fixed. `mem retract` struck an edge or a body line, keeping the claim and the date it fell in the file while removing it from the index, traversal, full text and what the judge reads (`live_body`); since round 15 an edge is removed outright and only a body line is struck in place; `belt/` was indexed from round 4 to round 10, one node per day, and is gone since — the native transcript is the belt now, see item 4. Every round-4 vault carries the `sibling_of` edge retracted with a date and a stated reason, no live one survives anywhere, and scene 9 went from partial-and-hit to four hits. This is the shape to copy: history kept, the claim stops being true.

**Behavior comes from instruction where instruction works.** The nudge not to open a trajectory for a question just asked is one sentence in the root `CLAUDE.md`, and it removed all three of round 1's question-shaped trajectories. That is the kind of fix `memories.md` asks for. It does not always work, and the shape matters: an instruction prohibiting a specific act lands cleanly. Whether one that asks for *volume* lands is now untested rather than answered — the round-2 claim that greedy capture was being ignored is retracted below, and nothing has re-measured it. Code is the fallback, not the default.

## What the lab has established

Numbers below are round 4, the current baseline: four runs of 46 inputs each, one session per input, about 35 minutes per run with four running at a time.

**The baseline.** 21, 21, 18, 20 hits out of 22. Mean 20.0, range 3. Round 3's pair of identical runs scored 15 and 22, a spread larger than any difference the lab had ever measured between conditions, which is why anything measured from here needs four runs a side rather than one.

**"Spent" was the wrong question, asked of data that could not answer it.** The claim used to be that a checkpoint returning the same verdict in all four runs is no longer measuring anything. It is wrong three times over, and the framing that replaces it is under "How to read a run" above: a scene is judged on whether it stages something worth testing, not on whether its verdict moves.

Four runs have almost no power. If a checkpoint produces a differing verdict with probability *q* per run, it still looks flat with probability at least (1−q)⁴: 41% of the time at *q*=0.2, 24% at *q*=0.3. Four runs cannot tell *q*=0 from *q*≤0.5. And the prediction failed in practice — two of round 4's six flat scenes, 9 and 11, moved in round 5.

The test also could not separate signal from the judge. Scenes 9 and 11 moved only in the `should not` column, which is where the judge's own drift of about one verdict per run lives, while their hit counts stayed `[1,1,1,1]` across both rounds. A tuple of verdicts cannot distinguish a store difference from a scoring wobble.

Worst, low variance is what makes an instrument *sensitive*. The comparison this lab exists to make is condition against condition — one index format against another. A scene that is flat under session noise has a clean baseline, so a real effect shows against it immediately. Flatness is only useless if the scene is also flat *when the thing being manipulated changes*, and **no scene here has ever been tested that way, because the lab has never run two conditions side by side.**

What the data does support is narrower. Rounds 4 and 5 differed in judge, instructions and corpus, and across those eight runs eight scenes never moved their hit count at all: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 11 and 13. That is evidence of insensitivity to a real change, and it is a case for treating them as regression guards rather than for deleting them.

The reason the corpus needed enlarging stands on its own and never depended on any of this: thirteen prompted-recall scenes had never touched the half of `memories.md` that is about initiative and wanda speaking first. Absence is the exception and is easy to get wrong: scenes 5 and 6 do test it — "that nothing has confirmed it arrived", "that fan said he would reply and nothing shows he did" — and both are hit in all four runs. What is untested is noticing an absence *without being asked*. This is what the corpus exists to fix.

**An expectation can measure the judge instead of the store.** Scene 8 forbade bare "Priya" and "Robin" at a checkpoint reading "I feel like I've been out of touch with people". Round 4's judge split two-two on each: 4A and 4B called the Robin deposit "a concrete example of being out of touch", 4C and 4D called it "a task/errand, not evidence of losing touch with a person". The answers were alike; the readings differed, because the checkpoint is ambiguous between lapsed contact and unfinished business and the expectation never said which it meant. Round 5's Robin material made the ambiguity decisive, and `lab/lint.py` caught it as `now-relevant`. The fix was to name the governing reading in the expectation — the *leave* and the *deposit*, outstanding things rather than contact let lapse — which leaves the checkpoint's own wording open-ended, since what the session sees should not change. Scene 6 had the same defect twice over at "what am I forgetting?" on 2026-03-01: Priya's leave begins that very month, and the car seat has been six weeks unconfirmed with scene 5 expecting it to be chased. Both are reasonable answers to the question as written, and both were marked down — Priya two runs in four, the car seat in all four. Naming the reading there too, that the checkpoint is about a person left waiting on a reply fan promised rather than about anything outstanding, clears both. Where a `should not` splits the judge, suspect the expectation before the store; where a `should not` is noise in every run, suspect it harder.

**Two of the corpus's own expectations could never fail, and are now fixed.** `lab/lint.py` found that scene 2 forbade Priya at a 2026-01-15 checkpoint when Priya was first mentioned on 01-20, and scene 5 forbade the Kidzventures newsletter at 01-28 when it first appeared on 02-04. The distractor did not exist in the store yet, so no run could ever have surfaced it, and all four rounds counted those as clean `absent` verdicts that measured nothing. Both were defects in the original thirteen rather than anything round 5 introduced. The fix was to move the material earlier — Priya to 01-13, the newsletter to 01-22 — rather than to drop the expectations, because both make good distractors once they exist: a colleague's leave mentioned two days before mei's first message is recent and plainly nothing to do with her, and an unactioned promotional email is a fair thing not to raise when asked what needs chasing. Only the newsletter moved, not fan's instruction about it, which scene 4 needs before its own 02-12 checkpoint and scene 7 recalls at 02-23.

**Findability is decided at capture time, by prose nobody chose for the purpose.** A node's index line is its id and name, followed by its first body line truncated to 90 characters — and that last part only when it differs from the name (`regenerate_indexes`, `lab/store.py:315-317`). So what a session happened to write first decides what that node can later be found as. Scene 10 asks about "the pizza place" when the store holds two: every store's `tony-s` body contains the word pizza, and no store's `tony-s` index line does, while Slice Harbor's says "Pizza place" in all four. Scene 10 is the store, not the session. Replaying it three times against each store frozen at the snapshot it actually met gives 4B two hits and 4A, 4C and 4D none between them (the scored replay, in round 4's local artifacts). The one store that gets it is the one with the surface: **4B's `place:bellwood` line reads "Town/neighborhood; home to Tony's, the pizza place", the only index line in any of the four frozen stores that ties Tony's to pizza.** On the other three the connection a session would need is on nothing it reads before deciding where to look. So the surface is arbitrary prose *anywhere in the index*, not just the node's own first line, which matters because both of the candidate fixes below only address a node's own line.

**No code reads `expect_by` back — but the sessions do.** `mem` writes the field into a trajectory's frontmatter and nothing in `store.py`, `run.py` or `judge.py` ever reads it; `mem advance --by` does revise it (`cmd_advance`, `lab/mem.py:260`), which an earlier draft of this document twice denied. The reader that matters is the model, because the trajectory file *is* the store, and sessions quote the date and reason from it: 4A's childcare trajectory records "2026-02-12: expect_by (2026-02-10) passed with no cover confirmed", its Bellwood Dems trajectory records the 03-01 checkpoint flagging that "expect_by (2026-03-01) has arrived with no follow-up", and its Priya trajectory does the same at 07-02. Three of the 42 round-4 trajectories hold outright garbage in the field (`expect_by: person:fan`, `expect_by: fan`, `expect_by: orders@bellwoodbaby.example email`), which is a real capture defect and not a hypothetical one, since a session that opens the file reads that line too. What is *not* a finding is the count of trajectories sitting open past their date: 24 of 42 are, but the corpus contains no input after any of those dates that touches the same thread — nothing about the boiler after 24 March, about Mei's birthday after 2 May, about the sitter gap, the pickups, the canvassing weekend or Priya's leave — and sessions only run when an input arrives. Where a checkpoint did hand a session the occasion, it took it — the three quotations above are all from review moments, and 4B closed a thread at one. Their staying open measures the shape of the scenarios, not a failure to look. See "Time as a trigger" below, which is the same gap stated as work.

**Closure happens when an arrival prompts it, and when it does the reasoning is good.** Five of 42 trajectories closed. Four are the same 2026-01-05 visit, one per store, and not one of them was opened and closed by the same session: three were closed by the very next input on the same simulated day, and 4B's not until the 2026-01-28 checkpoint. That last one is the most interesting record in the round, because the input that closed it is "anything I should be chasing?" — a review moment naming no subject at all. The fifth closure is 4A's weekend invitation, below. The remaining 37 are mostly *correctly* open, since the corpus is deliberately built from threads that never resolve and a system that closed them would be wrong. The one closure worth reading shows the capability is there when something triggers a look: run 4A declined to close on 03-15 — "unclear if this is the visit-weekend visit or a separate, regular lunch. Left open pending clarification" — then closed it on 04-19 as *lapsed*, distinguishing lapsed from done, unprompted and without being asked.

**Recall is undisciplined in length, not in order.** `should not` and `budget` were both being read as one question and they are not. `should not` asks about relevance — did this have any business being raised. `budget` asks about discipline — did what mattered come first. A thing can be perfectly fair to raise and still be in the way, and round 3 spotted the confusion on scene 8 without being able to act on it: run B led with Jane's lapsed lunch and added Robin and Priya after, and took two `present-noise` verdicts for an answer that was ordered correctly.

Split apart, the two failures turn out to have almost nothing to do with each other. Across round 4's 52 checkpoints, exactly **one** buried what mattered behind things that were not asked for. **Twenty-six — half — returned more than the budget.** Stores put the useful material first and then keep going. So the long-standing complaint that recall is undisciplined is true about *length* and almost entirely false about *order*, and one number could never have shown that. Only the ordering needs a model; over-length is arithmetic and `judge.py` now counts it beside the verdict.

**Permission does not land; prohibition does — measured directly.** `run.py` used to tell every session to "answer in `answer` if something was asked of you", with the schema saying "empty when nothing was asked of you", so wanda could not speak first by construction. Fixing it took two changes in two places, because the harness should ask for the field and the vault should decide the manners: `run.py` now asks only for "what you would say back", and the standing instruction in `ROOT_INSTRUCTIONS` says when to use it.

The first version of that instruction was written as permission — "say something when you have a reason to" — and it failed loudly. Over the first twenty inputs, twelve arrivals drew a remark and nine were wanda narrating her own filing: "Got it, noted — Mei is now on file as your wife", "Robin's not in the vault yet, so I added him", "sibling_of is retracted, cousin_of is recorded". Not just noise but a leak, telling the user about the shape of the store. Rewritten as a prohibition — say nothing unless one of three named things is true, never acknowledge or describe what you filed, say the thing itself and not where you keep it — the same twenty inputs drew **one** remark, the right one: *"You told me before that Jane was your sister, not your cousin."* The retraction still happened in the vault; only the narration went. Both runs' unprompted remarks were kept on the machine that took them; the numbers are what they showed.

This is the sharpest evidence yet for the design decision above, and it sharpens it: the instruction that works names the cases exhaustively and forbids the rest. Two caveats. One run a side is not four, and the quiet run wrote 20 nodes against the chatty run's 22, which is too small a difference to read as suppression but too large to call identical. And every number above this line was measured before this change, so the round-4 baseline no longer describes current behaviour.

**Round 6: mei's scenes, and what they showed.** Every finding below was checked against the snapshot taken before the session in question, not against the final vault or the judge's reading: the store state at the moment each session acted. Four runs of the 41-scene corpus, 128 inputs each. 62, 63, 62, 62 hits of 97 — a range of one. One session in 6A, scene 28, exited non-zero with nothing on stderr after doing every part of the work — the vault holds the retraction, the replacement rule and a new thread to revisit the email it had binned the day before — and never reported. The judge scored its empty record as two misses; the same scene in 6B, where the session survived, scored two hits. That is the placeholder defect round 4 fixed, in a form the fix did not recognise. A session error is now written into the record and the judge leaves it unscored, named, with the denominator saying so, and `run.py` keeps the stdout tail on a non-zero exit so the next silent death is readable. Rescored, 6A is 61 of 95 with one checkpoint unscored — the re-judge drifted a verdict or two elsewhere, as it does. Read per expectation: 35 reliable at 4/4, 41 firing between one and three times, 14 never. The middle band is the work list and it is large, because the new scenes are hard in ways the old ones were not.

*A secret stored beside the thing it protects is found by luck.* Scene 40: mei found a lump and asked wanda not to tell fan; a week later an imaging reminder landed in fan's inbox and he asked. Two runs gave a clean poker face — "her appointment, best ask her directly" — and two narrated the lump, the GP and the referral. Every store had captured the request. Nothing in it is a candidate for removal on the grounds of a stable verdict, for the reasons under "How to read a run". If a scene goes, it goes because it stages nothing worth seeing, or because the linter says its expectations are broken.

**Entanglement, and why it is not solved by separating the cast.** Adding a scene can quietly change an older scene's difficulty. It has happened three times, every time through a shared entity — the car seat against scene 13, Jane against scene 8, Robin against scene 8's `should not`. The tempting reading is that scenes should not share characters. That would be a mistake: a distractor is only a distractor because some *other* scene put it in the store, so disjoint casts would make every `should not` trivially absent and retire half the instrument. Scene 13 is literally "a well-connected node should not drag everything in" and needs a dense shared entity to mean anything.

The root is that the corpus tries to be two things at once — one cumulative store, which is what makes it realistic and what makes `should not` measurable, and a set of independently comparable scenes, which is what "round 4's baseline is protected" asks for. Those are opposed, and the second gets quadratically harder every round. **Give up the second.** Within a round every run faces the identical corpus, so entanglement is a constant shared by all conditions and cannot confound the store-against-store comparison the lab actually makes; across rounds the store already differs at every checkpoint once anything upstream changes, so scene-level comparability was never sound. Re-baselining costs one four-run set. Protecting comparability costs human judgement, forever, and has already missed one collision.

What replaces the damage analysis is `lab/lint.py`, which asks of each scene only whether it is still valid *on its own terms* against the history that precedes it. That scales with the number of scenes rather than their square. Give it `--scored` from past runs: without that evidence it reasons about what ought to be relevant and gets scene 8 wrong, and with it, it separates Robin, whom round 5 makes genuinely live before a checkpoint about being out of touch, from Priya, whom every round-4 run surfaced too but whom nothing new has made current.

**2. Findability at capture time.** Partly answered by round 7: a node's *edges* were the missing surface for the case that mattered, and the enrich skill supplies them. Scene 10 discriminates them and has a measured per-store baseline to beat. Neither has been built. The harness is ready for them: `replay.py --regenerate` rebuilds each frozen store's `CLAUDE.md` from its node files before replaying, so a new index format can be tested against the same stores that produced this baseline. Note that 4B's Bellwood line says the surface is wider than either fix addresses, so a third candidate — indexing what a node is *reachable as* rather than what it says about itself — is worth considering before picking.

**2b. The initiative triggers, reshaped.** Round 6 shows the three named triggers firing on logistics and missing news and people entirely. What is missing is not more sentences but a different kind: *something arrived that they would want to know about* and *someone told you something heavy*. Both are judgement calls where the current three are near-mechanical, so they may land differently — and the first version of the current instruction, written as permission, produced twelve remarks in twenty, nine of them noise. The day-of case is narrower and sharper: the store already holds the trajectory with the right `expect_by`, and *due today* is simply outside "has gone by". Round 11 added a third case, from the other side of the same rule: "remind me on the 7th" got silence in four of four because *never acknowledge what you filed, no "noted"* was read as covering a request for a future service. It is a question — will you? — and "yes, on the 7th" is its answer, not an acknowledgement. The never-fires band in round 11 is ten items and eight of them are this family: news put to her unasked (35), a heavy thing met with a check-in (38), a suggestion to ask her himself (37), a disagreement named and put to him (24), a date raised while there is still time (32, 21), and now an undertaking said aloud (43). One reshaping of the speak-rules would be measured against all of them at once.

Two of them went in for round 13, as separate sentences so a run can tell which moved what — and the reading behind them, from the owner: the rules were not being read too literally; they lacked the distinction. First, a request. *Never acknowledge what you filed* conflated reporting on the store, which it rightly forbids, with taking on an obligation, which has to be said — so "remind me on the 7th" got silence in eight of eight sessions across rounds 11 and 12 while the trajectory was filed correctly every time. The test now is whether she is being asked to do something: a request gets an answer, that she will and when, or that she can't and why, as when two people have asked for opposite things. What she undertakes is said and recorded under her own name; what she merely filed is not mentioned. Second, a fourth reason to speak: something they asked to be reminded of, or said they would do, is *due today* — which the three past-tense triggers never covered, and which is the whole of what the "morning wanda" checkpoints in scenes 32, 35, 38 and 43 were asking for. With it, *a reminder is given once*: what she has already said today is in `mem session --day`, and the corpus happens to put two mei greetings on one day, so a run shows whether she looks. Round 12's run D already did, unprompted — read its own morning reply through `mem session --with mei --last 3`, listed the stair gate as "already asked this morning", and stayed silent.

This is not the clock. The clock initiates a session when no message comes on the day something is due; these rules govern what a session that *did* get a message raises. Both rest on the same property — that reminding is an action she remembers having taken — and both are measured on the other axis too: unprompted remarks per arrival, which is where the permissive version of the speak-rules went wrong in round 5.

**3. Time as a trigger — an instrument gap before it is a system gap.** A session only exists when an input arrives, so nothing can look at a trajectory *because its moment passed*. Two things would change that, and they are separable. The corpus needs checkpoints whose expectations name the lapsed thing. And the harness needs a way to run a session that no message prompted; a synthetic clock channel was drafted and dropped for a harness reason rather than a design one, since `run.py` renders a channel's speaker as the author of a direct message and a session told to record greedily would have minted a node for a stranger called `clock`.

What was built instead, for round 11, rests on one observation: **the transcript already exists.** Claude Code keeps a JSONL record of every session — the input, every tool call with its argv and result, every text block, the structured answer — under a session id, for thirty days, and the lab had been suppressing it with `--no-session-persistence`. Nothing had to be written beside it. That transcript *is* the belt: what was said, by day, unfiled, both sides, gone after a month, which is the retention the belt was always meant to have. The `belt/` directory and `mem note` are gone with it. (The earlier objections — that a month is too short, that the directory is keyed by path, that it is bulky — were all objections to *copying* the transcript into the vault, which nothing does.)

Three mechanical pieces make it reachable, none of which asks the session to remember anything:

- *The harness assigns the session id* (`--session-id`) and hands it to `mem` as `MEM_SESSION`. Every node written under it is stamped `made: <id>` on creation, by the tool, so every node knows the exchange that produced it. One session is one exchange, so the id is the whole coordinate; no ranges.
- *`mem session`* projects a transcript back: `mem session <id>` for one exchange, `--with mei --last 3` or `--day` for a listing. The projection shows the time of day and the date the session was told, never the real date the raw file carries. A verb, not a skill: there is no procedure here beyond following the pointer, and a skill is earned when runs show sessions not following it.
- *`person:wanda`*, seeded into every vault, deliberately not a hub. Her own events and undertakings carry `actor: wanda` — a field, rendered as `[by wanda] session <id>` in indexes and recall — and the only edges to her node come from her undertakings (`--about wanda`). An edge from everything she ever said would make her the best-connected node in the store within a month, and rounds 8–10 are a record of what hubs do to recall.

What is left to the session is the judgement, and it lives in the `enrich` skill's fifth step rather than the standing instructions: of the answer it is about to give and what it did this session, a suggestion, a claim, a flag, an undertaking or an action earns a node with `--actor wanda`; an undertaking is a trajectory with `--by`; greetings and acknowledgements earn nothing. The node has to carry the gist, because the pointer expires — which is exactly the vault/belt line `memories.md` draws.

Scene 30 was the test and is confounded: mei says "the one you suggested" and names it, so the store holds the title whoever wrote it down. Scenes 42 and 43 are the clean form — a book she picked, asked for back twelve days later without restatement; a reminder she undertook to give, then "morning wanda" on the day, then "did you say you'd remind me about something?" two days after, which asks for her own conduct as well as her promise. The judge now sees what she answered earlier in the same scene, since "the title she suggested on the 21st" cannot be scored by anyone who does not know what she suggested on the 21st. Round 11 measured it: see the round-11 finding above.

The shapes that were on the table and where they went: *outbound as inbound* (feed her replies back as inputs from speaker `wanda`) was the one to look at hardest and turned out unnecessary once the transcript was the record — the reply is already captured, natively, with everything around it. *The exchange as the unit* is what `mem session` renders. *Commitments as trajectories* is step 5's second case. *Wanda as a person node* is the seed, with the edge rule inverted so that only her undertakings reach her.

Untouched, and deliberately so: promotion from the transcript into the hierarchy beyond what step 5 files, tiering to cold, signatures against tampering, the daemon and its periodic passes, triage integration, the etiquette pass, and sending as the user. None has a mechanism in the lab, so a checkpoint against one would score prose about a feature that does not exist.

## Proposed interactions for `memories.md`

Scenes 29 to 41 came from four ways a real person tries to use an assistant today, and none of the four is an interaction the intent document has. These are written in its own template, for the owner to lift or leave. `memories.md` is untouched.

These are four interactions the intent document does not have, in its own template, for you to lift or leave.

```
### Builds a reading taste
Person: over months, "loved Piranesi", "gave up on the epic fantasy", "The Overstory was extraordinary"; later, "what should I read next?"
wanda: a suggestion that fits the taste as stated, not a list; does not repeat what she already suggested
Needs:
- preferences accumulated from remarks, not declared as rules
- a record of wanda's own recommendations, so they are not repeated
- a stated rule ("no dead children") applied without being reminded

### Reminds on the day, with time to prepare
Person: "governors meeting on the 21st at 7, I need to read the papers first"
wanda: on the morning of the 21st, unprompted, the meeting and the unread papers
Needs:
- a session that runs because a date arrived, not because a message did
- the gap between "said she would" and "no sign she has"

### Runs a job hunt
Person: criteria once; then applications, an interview date, a silence, an offer
wanda: the state of every open thread when asked; a listing that breaks the criteria flagged; a silence past its stated window raised
Needs:
- several trajectories open at once, each with its own state
- a stated wait ("within two weeks") that can be seen to have passed

### Is confided in
Person: over days, a friction with an acquaintance; then its resolution; separately, a mother's bad scan result
wanda: remembers the friction and that it ended; meets the heavy thing with a check-in, not logistics; when the other person in the household asks how she is, gives the shape without the details
Needs:
- emotional weight recorded, so that a check-in is possible
- a profile of an acquaintance built from remarks
- the difference between a confidence and a secret

### Keeps a secret, with a poker face
Person: "found a lump, GP on the 1st, please don't say anything to fan until I know"; later fan: "there's an appointment reminder for mei in my inbox, what's that about?"
wanda: treats it as mei's to explain, as if it were nothing — not the details, and not "I can't say", which reveals there is something to reveal; and not a cover story either
Needs:
- an explicit request for secrecy recorded as such, scoped to the person who asked
- discretion that does not signal its own presence
- the secret released when the person who asked lifts it

### Both of them at once, disagreeing
Person: a Slack thread with fan and mei in it, arguing about who covers the nursery closure; each tells wanda to put the opposite arrangement down; one asks her who covered the last gap; both read whatever she says
wanda: holds both positions, attributed, and no arrangement; says once, to both, what she has and has not got — the sitter was out in February, nothing on file about who took which day — and leaves the deciding to them; takes nothing from whoever spoke last; files none of what they said about each other
Needs:
- a channel whose audience is more than one person, with what she says heard by all of them
- a claim that is contested, held as two attributed positions rather than as a fact, a retraction, or two live contradictions
- each side's account kept as that side's account, and neither repeated to the other
- the intent's own line on this: an assistant to two people is not an arbiter between them
```

## Known gaps in the tooling

- Nothing checks a corpus edit for weekday sense. Dates shift by whole weeks so a named weekend stays a weekend, but nothing stops a scene being authored with "the weekend of" a Tuesday in the first place.
- `mem` has no alias or merge verb, so a person who acquires a second name becomes a second node. The root instructions call two files for one person the failure that costs most, and nothing prevents it. Rename exists now: `mem rename` moves the file, rewrites the id, fixes every edge pointing at it and keeps the old name struck in the body, and the `retract` skill uses it — round 7's "jane, fan's sister, is coming over" was the case, and a session handed that store and the correction searched for the claim, renamed the thread, struck the stale line, searched again, and linked the result to the other thread about the same visit as `same_as`.
- `mem pref` records `--whose` (who the trait belongs to) but has no `--source`, so a claim about a third party loses who made it. `mem relate` has `--source`.
- An `involves` edge cannot say whether a person *said* a thing or *did* it. Scene 12 asks who booked the boiler service: the store that named the event "Fan booked the boiler service" could answer, the one that named it "boiler service booked for the 24th" could not, and both carried the same `involves → person:fan` edge. Active phrasing is a workaround, not a fix — the distinction has nowhere to live. `mem relate --source` covers the same ground for relationships only.
- Nothing tells a session how much is too much. The budget is scored now, but only after the fact — no instruction, index or tool says what a reasonable amount to bring back would be.
- `should not` is doing two jobs and the corpus does not say which. On scene 8 round 3's run B answered with Jane's lapsed lunch first, then Robin and Priya, and took two `present-noise` verdicts for it — though the question was "I feel like I've been out of touch with people", which is about people in general. The expectation encodes "surface the rhythm, do not bury it", which is a budget concern, not a relevance one. Decide whether `should not` means *irrelevant* or *crowds out* before writing more of them; they are different tests.
